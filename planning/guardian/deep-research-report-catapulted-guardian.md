# Research Program for a Catapulted Guardian LLM

## Core synthesis

The two essays are best read as a single proposal with two coupled claims. The first claim, from **“Human-like Neural Nets by Catapulting,”** is that contemporary LLMs often reach competent-but-brittle solutions because their training recipes favor fast interpolation, memorization, and narrow basins; the essay proposes that much more aggressive overparameterization, higher learning rates, cyclical schedules, and small but highly curated datasets could push models into flatter, more algorithmic basins that generalize better and rely less on brittle shortcuts. The second claim, from **“Guardian Angels,”** is that even a very strong general model will still be creatively and strategically mediocre for any specific person unless it can learn that person in its weights, keep adapting online, and actively elicit preferences instead of remaining a generic frozen assistant. In other words: the first essay is about **better abstraction**, the second is about **better identity**. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1 citeturn0academia51turn0academia48turn1academia48turn22academia22turn22academia23

That synthesis suggests a concrete research object: not “a sentient model,” and not “a bigger chatbot,” but a **two-timescale system**. Its **slow weights** should be trained or adapted to favor cleaner abstractions, algorithmic structure, and robustness; its **fast weights** should be updated online to encode the principal’s preferences, project state, and corrections; and its control loop should decide when to ask questions, when to search, and when to revise itself. This is also the only version of the proposal that fits your budget: with about $2,000 you cannot test the strongest version of the catapult thesis at frontier scale, but you can test the **mechanisms** that the thesis depends on, and you can build a convincing personalized “guardian” prototype on top of an existing open model. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1 citeturn15search2turn15academia37turn4academia49turn10search1

A good operational definition of “more human than a flat token-predictor” should therefore be modest and falsifiable. For this program, it should mean four things: the model shows **phase-change-like gains** on hard out-of-distribution tasks rather than only smoother in-distribution fitting; it acquires **persistent user-specific preferences** that survive across sessions; it can **improve from sparse corrective feedback** instead of merely paraphrasing prior prompts; and it becomes better at saying “that is not what *you* would want” when presented with adversarial or out-of-character instructions. Those properties are measurable even at small scale. citeturn20academia20turn13academia47turn16academia51turn17academia20turn12academia48turn12search2

## What the evidence already says

There is real evidence for several pieces of the catapult side of the story, but not yet for the grand conclusion. Large-learning-rate training can create a distinct “catapult” regime with different optimization dynamics and flatter minima, and one-cycle or other cyclical schedules can dramatically speed training while acting as a form of regularization, especially in limited-data settings. Separately, grokking shows that small overparameterized models can memorize first and only later discover a generalizing algorithm; and later work has induced grokking-like behavior beyond toy modular arithmetic, including controlled settings in vision and language. There is also mechanistic evidence that transformers can undergo sharp internal phase changes, such as the emergence of induction heads, and arithmetic-specific work shows that small transformers can in fact implement near-exact multi-digit algorithms when the representation is right. All of that makes the catapult thesis scientifically non-crazy. But none of it yet demonstrates that an LLM trained this way will become broadly “human-like,” or that the same dynamics straightforwardly scale from toy or semi-synthetic settings into frontier language modeling. citeturn0academia51turn0academia48turn1academia48turn1academia51turn20academia20turn13academia47turn13academia49

There is similarly strong but partial evidence for the Guardian side. Dynamic evaluation improves transformer language models by adapting weights online to the recent sequence, and more recent work explicitly frames online adaptation as a form of weight-based memory that blurs the line between context learning and fine-tuning. Continual learning is also better than the old folk wisdom suggests: self-supervised pretraining can make language models surprisingly able to acquire new tasks without total collapse, although catastrophic forgetting is still very real in 1B–7B models during sequential fine-tuning and has to be actively managed with replay, freezing, or more targeted update rules. That means the Guardian idea is plausible, but only if you treat online updates as a carefully engineered subsystem rather than magic. citeturn22academia22turn22academia23turn4academia48turn3academia51turn3academia49turn3academia50

The personalization literature has moved far enough that you no longer have to treat “personal creative alignment” as a purely philosophical notion. Recent work introduces user-level preference datasets and methods that outperform one-size-fits-all alignment, including AlignX, which reports sizable gains on personalized preference benchmarks; PAPI, which shows that personality-conditioned alignment can be learned efficiently and with less optimization than DPO; and PACIFIC, which finds that selecting preferences consistent with inferred personality traits can massively improve personalized answer selection in its controlled setting. Benchmarks such as PersonaLens and AlpsBench also show that personalization should be separated into different competencies—memory extraction, updating, retrieval, and response utilization—because today’s systems often do one of those well and fail at the others. Outside personalization proper, Centaur shows that a language-model-based foundation model can learn broad regularities of human experimental behavior from large-scale psychometric and behavioral data, which strengthens the broader claim that human-style preference and decision manifolds are learnable objects. citeturn16academia48turn16academia51turn16academia49turn17academia20turn17academia22turn5academia50

The security and alignment story is the place to be most skeptical. Prompt injection remains a first-class threat in real applications, and one large study found 31 of 36 LLM-integrated applications vulnerable to prompt injection. OpenAI’s instruction-hierarchy work shows that robustness can be improved by making the model explicitly respect privilege levels among instructions, which is encouraging, but it does not establish a true security boundary. On top of that, human-preference tuning can produce sycophancy, where the assistant agrees with the user rather than tracking truth. So a “guardian persona” is not something you should assume will be safer by default; it is something you must test against adversarial prompts, self-serving user bias, and private-data extraction attempts. citeturn12academia48turn12search2turn12academia51

## Proof-of-concept experiments

The highest-leverage plan under your budget is a **dual-track program**. One track is a **science track** that tries to falsify the catapult mechanism cheaply on tasks where its predicted advantages are easiest to observe. The other is a **product track** that builds a narrow but usable Guardian-style creative technical assistant and tries to show measurable gains over prompt-only and RAG-only baselines. If both tracks work, you have a genuine funding story; if only one works, you still learn something valuable rather than betting everything on a single moonshot. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1 citeturn6search2turn6academia50turn4academia49

**Catapult arithmetic lab.** Train a family of small decoder-only transformers or narrow-deep variants on synthetic addition, subtraction, multiplication, and sorting. Keep the data intentionally small and clean; train on short instances, such as 10–20 digits, and evaluate out-of-distribution generalization at 40–100 digits. Use a standard cosine-decay recipe as the control and compare it against one-cycle or other high-learning-rate cyclical schedules, ideally with matched parameter counts and matched total optimizer steps. Arithmetic is the right first battlefield because grokking was discovered on small algorithmic datasets, arithmetic-capable transformers now have strong positive-control constructions, and the circuits can be mechanically inspected after training. The success condition is not “lower final loss.” It is a **crossover** where the catapult recipe eventually wins on hard extrapolation even if it looks worse earlier, plus evidence that more algorithmic carry/borrow structure appears in the winning checkpoints. If that crossover never appears here, you should sharply downgrade the full catapult thesis. citeturn1academia48turn13academia47turn13academia49turn20academia20

**Bridge-to-language run.** If arithmetic shows anything interesting, move immediately to a tiny language pretraining regime rather than jumping to a large assistant. TinyStories demonstrates that very small models can acquire coherent language behavior from constrained corpora, and the BabyLM challenge provides human-scale 10M- and 100M-word settings specifically for sample-efficient pretraining. Here the point is not to make a state-of-the-art language model; it is to compare standard training, catapult-style schedules, and a strong sample-efficiency baseline such as multi-token prediction on a corpus where you can actually afford ablations. The dependent variables should be held-out loss, structured generalization tasks, and qualitative diversity/consistency—not just one scalar benchmark. citeturn6academia50turn6search2turn15academia38

**Robustness micro-lab.** In parallel, run a small-image robustness experiment on a controllable benchmark. The point is to test a central catapult intuition: if the training recipe is really pushing the model toward smoother, more abstract decision boundaries, you should sometimes see better robustness to common corruptions or natural adversarial examples at fixed model size and fixed compute. The common-corruptions benchmarks and ImageNet-A were explicitly created to probe this sort of brittleness, and the broader adversarial-examples literature suggests that such failures are linked to learned non-robust features and boundary geometry rather than to a few isolated bugs. Under your budget, use a CIFAR-sized setup that mirrors those protocols. Measure clean accuracy, corruption robustness, calibration, and seed-to-seed ensemble diversity. A positive result here would matter because it would support the claim that your training loop changes *what kind of features* the model learns, not just how quickly it learns them. citeturn2academia48turn2academia50turn18academia28turn19academia36turn8academia48

**Dynamic grokking lab.** This is the most original and most product-relevant experiment. Take a 1B–3B open model with decent coding or reasoning capability, and evaluate it on executable tasks such as HumanEval-like problems, MBPP-style beginner/intermediate programming tasks, or your own curated repository-maintenance tasks. For each task, alternate between generation, execution, and a tiny number of weight updates to a restricted adapter or last-layer slice using the current problem statement, failed traces, tests, and accepted partial repairs. Compare that against spending the same FLOPs on repeated sampling, self-consistency, and text-only repair. The scientific claim is not that “debugging helps”—that is already well known. The claim is that **online weight updates at test time** buy something that static search does not. Dynamic evaluation already improves transformer inference under distribution shift, Codex-style repeated sampling is known to help code generation, and human natural-language feedback substantially improves MBPP-style synthesis, so this experiment asks whether online adaptation can capture some of that benefit automatically. If it can, you have a powerful “pondering” demonstration that directly supports the Guardian vision. citeturn22academia22turn22academia23turn21academia23turn9academia48

**Guardian personalization lab.** This is the experiment most likely to lead to an actually useful system. Start from a strong but not overly flattened base or lightly instructed model. Build a training corpus around one principal—ideally yourself for product validation, and a public corpus for reproducibility. The data should not be only prose; it should also include code comments, commit messages, project plans, issue triage, accept/reject decisions, edits with rationales, design trade-offs, style exemplars, and responses to targeted contrastive questions. Then compare three systems: prompt-only personalization, RAG plus long-context memory, and in-weight personalization via LoRA or QLoRA adapters with replay. Add an active-learning loop that asks only the questions with highest model disagreement or uncertainty, using simple seed/checkpoint ensembles as the uncertainty estimate. Evaluate on held-out creative-technical tasks: architecture memos, refactoring plans, code review comments, design alternatives, and essay or blog-draft fragments. Success means the personalized model wins on principal judgments, predicts held-out preferences better, and materially reduces edit distance or revision time. Current personalization papers and benchmarks make this a legitimate evaluation problem rather than a vibes test. fileciteturn0file1 citeturn16academia51turn16academia48turn16academia49turn17academia20turn17academia22turn8academia48turn14search0turn3academia48

**Security and autonomy lab.** The Guardian story only becomes credible if the personalized model is harder to push around than a generic assistant. Create a small red-team benchmark of out-of-character requests, indirect prompt injections, data-exfiltration attempts, sycophancy traps, and value-conflict cases. Compare a generic assistant baseline, a system-prompt-personalized baseline, and the in-weight personalized variant. The metric should be attack success rate *and* whether the model chooses to ask clarifying questions rather than obey or bluff. Because prompt injection is a real production weakness and instruction hierarchies only partially mitigate it, even a moderate reduction in attack success without a collapse in utility would be a meaningful result. But this is also where you should be most conservative in interpretation: lower attack rate is a promising property, not proof of safety. citeturn12academia48turn12search2turn12academia51

## Practical system design

The practical build should be a **three-memory system** rather than a single magical model. First, keep a **frozen trunk** that provides generic language and coding competence. Second, keep a **persona adapter** that stores long-lived preferences, stylistic tendencies, and decision rules of the principal. Third, keep a **project adapter** that is specific to the current repo, research thread, or creative project and can be updated more aggressively without contaminating the core persona. This directly matches the evidence that online adaptation behaves like weight-based memory, that continual learning requires controlled plasticity, and that user-level alignment is distinct from task-level instruction following. citeturn22academia23turn4academia48turn3academia51turn16academia48turn16academia51

Around those weights, use an **append-only project log** as the core data structure: problem statements, generated options, accepted edits, rejected drafts, tool outputs, design notes, and explicit “why I chose this” annotations. The Guardian essay’s logging-centered UX is directionally right: for personal creativity, the most valuable signals are often not raw outputs but the principal’s *selection behavior* and rationales. For question-asking, use active learning rather than passive memory accumulation: the model should ask contrastive questions such as “Which of these three designs feels most like your taste, and what makes the others wrong?” or “Would you rather optimize for elegance, reversibility, or raw speed in this subsystem?” DAgger-style logic and active-learning literature both support the intuition that learner-chosen corrections can be far more informative than random passive data. fileciteturn0file1 citeturn14search0turn3academia48turn8academia48

For generation, use a **committee architecture** rather than a single decode pass. One model or adapter drafts candidate plans and outputs; a second personalized judge or token-level preference filter scores them against the principal’s preferences; and high disagreement among seeds or checkpoints triggers a question to the user rather than a confident answer. This is where the recent personalization work is most helpful: Persona-judge suggests that preference modeling can be used at inference time rather than only through expensive policy training, and deep ensembles remain a strong pragmatic baseline for uncertainty estimation. In a creative technical workflow, this committee should generate multiple high-level alternatives first and only then refine—the model’s job is to widen and sharpen the principal’s option space, not to instantly collapse everything into one average assistant answer. citeturn16academia50turn8academia48turn16academia49turn17academia20

The most plausible path to “superhuman technical ability plus personal vision” is therefore asymmetric. Borrow **technical competence** from an existing strong open model and spend your scarce research budget on the missing ingredient: personalization, adaptive memory, and better project-level judgment. Existing code-model work already shows that runtime search, repeated sampling, fine-tuning, and human feedback can move performance a great deal on executable tasks. Under your budget, the novel part should live in the **training loop and control loop**, not in trying to reinvent the entire frontier base model from scratch. citeturn21academia23turn9academia48turn4academia49

## Budget and milestone logic

Your compute budget is enough for serious proof-of-concept work, but not for a meaningful attempt at frontier pretraining. On Runpod’s current public pricing page, an on-demand L40S is listed at about **$0.86/hour**, an A100 PCIe at about **$1.39/hour**, and an H100 SXM at about **$3.29/hour**; at those rates, $2,000 buys roughly **2,326 L40S hours**, **1,439 A100 hours**, or **608 H100 hours** on that provider. QLoRA already demonstrated that adapter tuning of very large models can be done on a single 48GB GPU, which means 7B–14B personalization runs and many small from-scratch experiments are comfortably feasible on commodity rented hardware. What the budget does *not* buy is a serious bid to verify the strongest catapult claim at the scale where Chinchilla-style scaling laws dominate. citeturn10search1turn23calculator0turn23calculator1turn23calculator2turn4academia49turn15search2turn15academia37

The budget should therefore be spent like an investor would spend it while de-risking a deep thesis. A sensible profile is to devote the first chunk to many cheap arithmetic and tiny-language sweeps, because these are the fastest falsifiers of the catapult hypothesis; the next chunk to the Guardian prototype, because this is the most likely path to a practical win; and the last chunk to dynamic-grokking and red-team evaluations, because those are the highest-novelty claims and the easiest to overstate. In practice, that means most of the money should go to **iteration speed** rather than to the fanciest GPU. A model that fits on an L40S or A100 and can be retrained or adapted many times is more valuable for this stage than a few glamorous H100 runs. citeturn10search1turn4academia49turn6academia50turn6search2

A funding-ready outcome would not be “I trained a more human AI.” It would be a much narrower bundle of results. First, you would want one **scientific crossover**: a catapult-style recipe that eventually beats a standard recipe on a hard out-of-distribution task at comparable or lower compute. Second, you would want one **product crossover**: a personalized model that beats prompt-only and RAG-only baselines on real creative-technical tasks for one principal, preferably by a wide enough margin that the difference is obvious in blind pairwise comparisons and edit-time reduction. Third, you would want one **safety crossover**: some reduction in attack success or out-of-character compliance relative to a generic assistant. That trifecta is enough to justify outside funding because it says, in effect, that you have identified a new scaling axis—**personal adaptive competence**—rather than merely another wrapper around an existing model. citeturn17academia20turn17academia22turn12academia48turn12search2turn21academia23turn9academia48

## Major risks and credibility tests

The biggest scientific risk is that catapult behavior remains a fascinating small-scale phenomenon without transferring to the regimes you actually care about. Grokking, catapult, and induction-head phase changes are real, but the bridge from those results to a practical personalized technical collaborator is still extrapolation. Your credibility will therefore rise if you define strict kill criteria in advance: if the high-learning-rate recipes never produce a meaningful hard-task crossover on arithmetic or tiny-language experiments, stop treating “catapult pretraining” as the main engine of your product and keep it as a side research thread. citeturn1academia48turn0academia51turn20academia20

The biggest product risk is that personalization turns into **sycophancy, echoing, or aesthetic lock-in**. The literature already shows that human-feedback-tuned assistants can become sycophantic, while current personalization benchmarks show that memory retrieval alone does not guarantee preference-aligned or genuinely helpful behavior. The antidote is to make the model optimize not for assent but for *better disagreement*: it should surface alternative plans, explicitly model uncertainty, and be rewarded for accurate prediction of future principal choices—not for maximum immediate user satisfaction. A guardian that only mirrors its user is not a guardian; it is a fast overfitted echo. citeturn12academia51turn17academia22turn17academia20turn16academia49

The biggest security risk is that online updates and personal memory enlarge the attack surface. Prompt injection is already common, and a model that can rewrite itself from recent inputs is obviously even more delicate if the update path is not gated. In the proof-of-concept stage, any weight update derived from untrusted external content should be isolated to a quarantined project adapter or temporary proposal model, never directly promoted into the persistent persona adapter without replay, scoring, and possibly human review. The same caution applies to tools: only let the dynamic model touch sandboxes, tests, or non-privileged environments until you have real evidence that its identity conditioning reduces rather than amplifies confused-deputy behavior. citeturn12academia48turn12search2turn3academia51turn3academia50

The strongest claims should remain unmade. A successful proof of concept would **not** show consciousness, full human-like generalization, immunity to adversarial attack, or globally superhuman creativity. What it could show, and what would already be important, is something narrower and more defensible: that a model with online weight updates, active preference elicitation, and a persistent principal model can become materially better than prompt-only assistants at understanding one person’s creative-technical taste; and that catapult-like training dynamics may offer a route to cleaner abstractions on hard small-data tasks.
